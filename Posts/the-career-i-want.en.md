I want children and a family, and I also want a career that excites me and is worth investing myself in. I have always thought these two desires would inevitably collide at a certain age: in midlife, we are expected to push full speed ahead at work while raising children and caring for aging parents. It feels as though everything important happens at the same time. Just thinking about it leaves me physically and mentally exhausted. No wonder birth rates are falling everywhere.

In China, I have seen the circumstances of children left behind while their parents work away from home, separated from them for long periods. I have had a similar experience myself. When my father went to work in the city, my mother stayed by my side, and I am grateful for that. Precisely because I know what her presence meant to me, I hope that when I have children, I can be as involved in their growing up as possible.

I was deeply inspired when I read Anne-Marie Slaughter’s discussion of careers ([“Why Women Still Can’t Have It All”](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2012/07/why-women-still-cant-have-it-all/309020/)). She suggests that we do not have to squeeze promotions, positions of authority, and the most demanding work into our middle years. A career can be longer and can have different stages: building skills and credentials when we are young; seeking greater control over our time during the years when our children need more care; and taking on more challenging roles as they become increasingly independent.

> They build her credentials and establish herself, at least in her first career, between 22 and 35;
> she will have children, if she wants them, sometime between 25 and 45; **she’ll want maximum flexibility and control over her time in the 10 years that her children are 8 to 18;** and she should plan to take positions of maximum authority and demands on her time after her children are out of the house.

This is not giving up on a career, but changing its pace.

This is also the career model I have been thinking about. In modern society, we marry and have children later, but we also live longer. The years in which we enjoy good health have extended too. We should adjust how we define career success accordingly.

> The American definition of a successful professional is someone who can climb the ladder the furthest in the shortest time, generally peaking between ages 45 and 55. It is a definition well suited to the mid-20th century, an era when people had kids in their 20s, stayed in one job, retired at 67, and were dead, on average, by age 71.
>
> It makes far less sense today. Average life expectancy for people in their 20s has increased to 80; men and women in good health can easily work until they are 75. They can expect to have multiple jobs and even multiple careers throughout their working life. Couples marry later, have kids later, and can expect to live on two incomes. They may well retire *earlier* —the average retirement age has gone down from 67 to 63—but that is commonly “retirement” only in the sense of collecting retirement benefits. Many people go on to “encore” careers.

I started working in my early twenties, and I do not want the kind of retirement where I have nothing to do. I want to always have a career to pursue. It helps keep my mind active (preventing dementia), maintain my social connections, and give me the sense of accomplishment that comes from contributing. I think that even at 70 or 80, I will still want to do things—but things I enjoy. When you do something you love, it does not feel like work at all, so what would retirement even mean?

I want that kind of career.

Psychotherapy, for example, is a field in which you become more valued as you grow older. Having life experience is an advantage in doing the work. And helping people brings a sense of accomplishment.

> Peaking in your late 50s and early 60s rather than your late 40s and early 50s makes particular sense for women, who live longer than men.

## Role models

Esther Perel is a role model I aspire to follow. In her sixties, she is still practicing psychotherapy, writing, and making podcasts, sharing her many years of experience working with couples and families with more people. She shows me that in some careers, being younger does not necessarily give you an advantage: as your life experience grows, your understanding of people may deepen too. I hope that when I reach her age, I will still be working with curiosity and using the experience I have accumulated to help others.

A Chinese psychotherapist I worked with last year is also in her sixties. She is helping so many families whose children have mental health issues.

Warren Buffett is still working in his nineties. He remains active in the investment field he loves. Although he has stepped down as CEO of Berkshire Hathaway (at **95**), he remains chairman. To me, he is a reminder that a career does not have to end abruptly at a certain age. As we grow older, we can also adjust our roles and the pace of our work, and continue doing meaningful things.

To balance her career and raising children, Michelle Obama once chose a job closer to home and limited her involvement during her husband’s presidential campaign so that she could leave more time for her daughters. She did not give up her career; she deliberately adjusted its pace during the years when her children needed her. She is now **62**. Both of her daughters are grown and have long since moved beyond the stage of needing daily care from their parents. She did not “retire” after her time as First Lady. In recent years, she has continued writing books, giving speeches, and taking part in projects such as podcasts.
